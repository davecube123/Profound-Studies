Twin primes: order or chance?

The primes follow a rule with no randomness in it. Yet if you pretend each number n is prime with probability 1/ln n, then correct for the fact that primes are odd, you can predict how many twin pairs (p, p+2) there are with startling accuracy. That prediction is still unproven.

π₂(x) ≈ 2·C₂·∫₂ˣ dt/(ln t)² ,   C₂ = ∏p≥3 p(p−2)/(p−1)² ≈ 0.66016

3 · Gaps between consecutive primes

Gaps of 6, 12 and 18 stand out. A number that is ≡ 1 or 5 (mod 6) can be prime, so primes avoid multiples of 2 and 3, and gaps that are multiples of 6 keep both residues "safe". This is the same kind of correction that gives C₂.

4 · The Ulam spiral: twins in the plane

Write 1, 2, 3, … in a square spiral. Grey dots are primes, and the highlighted ones belong to a twin pair. The diagonal streaks come from quadratic polynomials that are unusually rich in primes (Euler's n² + n + 41 is the famous example). That is structure hiding inside "randomness".

Questions

  • Why must every twin pair after (3, 5) be of the form (6k−1, 6k+1)?
  • The naive model underestimates by a factor of 2C₂ ≈ 1.32. Derive that factor from the prime 2 (and then 3, 5, …).
  • Brun proved the sum of 1/p over twin primes converges (to ≈ 1.902). So twins are "rare". Yet we expect infinitely many. How can both be true?
