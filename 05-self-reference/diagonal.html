One picture, three revolutions

Cantor (1891), Gödel (1931) and Turing (1936) all used the same move: list everything, walk down the diagonal, do the opposite. Learn this picture once and you've seen the core of all three.

The shared skeleton

1 · Assume a complete listEvery sequence, every program, every provable fact…
2 · Look at the diagonalRow i, column i: each thing applied to itself. This is where self-reference enters.
3 · Flip itBuild a new object that disagrees with row i at position i, for every i.
4 · It can't be in the listIf it were row k, cell (k,k) would have to equal its own opposite.

Cantor: there are more real numbers than whole numbers. Turing: no program decides halting. Gödel: the flipped row is a sentence rather than a contradiction. It is true, but the system can't prove it. Complexity theory reuses the same picture for the time-hierarchy theorem: more time lets you solve strictly more.
